Bell-Bottom Bob by Face Shape

A bob with a relatively smooth upper section and ends styled to flare outward.

The bell-bottom bob combines a close upper section with ends that bend outward. The flare is a styled finish as well as a silhouette. Decide where that added width should sit and whether you want to recreate it regularly.

Bell-Bottom Bob variations for seven face shapes

Use these suggestions to choose where the haircut adds width or leaves the outline visible. You can prefer a different effect.

Oval face

Choose a small outward bend near the jaw or just below it; exaggerated flips need more daily styling.

Round face

Keep the upper section close and place a restrained flare below the chin if you want less side width.

Square face

Place the curved ends below the jaw corner and avoid a rigid horizontal shelf.

Oblong face

A shorter bob with some side fullness may reduce the impression of length; keep crown height modest.

Heart face

Let the ends flare near the narrower lower face while keeping the upper section smooth.

Diamond face

Put the outward bend below the cheekbones so it adds lower-face width rather than more cheek width.

Triangle face

Consider a small bend below the jaw or a softer layered bob; a large flare on the jaw can emphasize its width.

Choose where the ends turn outward

A flare beside the jaw draws a horizontal line across that part of the face. Moving the ends below the jaw changes where the eye reads the width. Compare a small bend with a larger flip; a few centimeters of length can change the effect.

If you want the upper outline to stay close, ask how your natural texture behaves there. A cut that expands near the cheeks may not resemble a reference with a smooth upper section unless you style it that way.

Separate the cut from the finish

A bob does not permanently hold an outward bend just because the ends are cut at a certain length. Ask the stylist to demonstrate the finish with tools you actually own, then discuss what the same cut looks like when you skip that step.

If you have strong waves or curls, an air-dried bob may have bends in several directions. You can keep that movement, choose a subtler flare, or select a layered bob instead. The straight-hair reference should not set an expectation for every texture.

Test the routine before choosing a dramatic flare

Show the stylist how much time you want to spend after washing and on the following day. A large, symmetrical flip may ask for more heat styling and touch-ups than a soft outward bend. This is why the finder places the polished bell-bottom version in its higher-maintenance group.

Check how the ends interact with your shoulders and collars. If the style only sits the way you want when every section is freshly styled, consider a more relaxed variation rather than relying on the haircut name to do that work.

Questions about bell-bottom bob

Does a bell-bottom bob suit a heart-shaped face?

A lower flare is one way to add width near a narrow chin. Its size and position still need to work with your hair, and you can choose a smaller bend if you prefer less width.

Is it suitable for a wide jaw?

It can be, if you like emphasizing that part of the outline. If you want less width there, compare a restrained bend below the jaw or a softer layered bob.

Is this a new haircut that only works in 2026?

No. A flared bob is a styling option you can use at any time. This guide focuses on length and routine rather than claiming a particular year's popularity.

Before your appointment

  • Bring a photo of your usual, unstyled hair.
  • Choose the length you need for tying or tucking back.
  • Ask to see how the ends sit when dry.
  • Discuss styling time and regular trims.

Maintenance: high. The outward bend is a styling step, not just a cut. It may need a round brush or heat tool each time you reset the style.
